What is Reta?

Retatrutide is a synthetic peptide engineered to mimic and combine the actions of three gut and pancreatic hormones involved in appetite, glucose control and energy use.

GIP

Glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide

Supports insulin secretion after meals and may influence fat metabolism and tolerability of the other signals.

GLP-1

Glucagon-like peptide-1

Reduces appetite, slows gastric emptying and improves glucose-dependent insulin release. The target of semaglutide.

Glucagon

Glucagon receptor

Believed to raise energy expenditure and reduce liver fat — the component that sets Reta apart from dual agonists like tirzepatide.

What the research shows

In a 2023 phase 2 trial of adults with obesity (Jastreboff et al., New England Journal of Medicine), once-weekly retatrutide produced dose-dependent weight loss at 48 weeks. Results below are mean percent change from baseline body weight.

Doses above are clinical-trial regimens, shown for context only. They are not recommendations. Participants also showed improvements in several cardiometabolic markers, and the trial reported gastrointestinal side effects, dose-related heart-rate increases and some dose-related discontinuations.

Where Reta stands today

A rough timeline of the development path. Check ClinicalTrials.gov and Lilly’s announcements for current status.

  1. Phase 1: early safety and pharmacokinetic studies in healthy volunteers and people with type 2 diabetes.
  2. Phase 2: obesity and type 2 diabetes studies published in 2023, showing substantial weight and glycemic effects.
  3. Phase 3 (TRIUMPH): large trials in obesity, obstructive sleep apnea, knee osteoarthritis and cardiovascular and kidney outcomes.
  4. Regulatory review: not yet approved by the FDA, EMA or any other major regulator.

Safety & the “research peptide” problem

Because interest in Reta is high, products labeled “retatrutide” are sold online as “research chemicals.” This is a real risk.

Important: Retatrutide is not approved for human use anywhere. Products sold online outside of clinical trials are unregulated: identity, purity, sterility and dose are unverified, and regulators have issued warnings about unapproved compounded and “research use only” GLP-1-class products.

Known or reported effects in trials include nausea, diarrhea, vomiting, decreased appetite and increased heart rate. Anyone interested in this class of treatment should talk to a licensed clinician about approved options, or look into enrolling in a registered clinical trial.

FAQ

Is Reta FDA-approved?

No. Retatrutide is still being studied in phase 3 trials. It is not approved by the FDA or any other major regulator.

Why is it called a “triple agonist”?

It activates three receptors — GIP, GLP-1 and glucagon — whereas semaglutide targets one and tirzepatide targets two.

Can I buy it legally?

Not as an approved medicine. Sellers offering it online are not authorized to supply it for human use, and quality cannot be assured. Approved alternatives such as semaglutide or tirzepatide are available by prescription.

Where can I follow the research?

Search “retatrutide” on ClinicalTrials.gov and PubMed, and follow Eli Lilly’s pipeline updates.
